*Why Index-Level Returns Mask the Most Significant Sector Divergence in a Generation* 

**T H E S I S S TAT E M E N T** 

A "Great Rotation" is underway in global equity markets. Capital is migrating from consumer-facing, rate sensitive, and low-growth businesses toward hard-asset producers, physical infrastructure builders, AI enablers, and defence contractors. The S\&P 500 headline index, by design, averages the rocketships with the sinking ships. This report identifies nine companies positioned on the winning side of this structural shift.

**WHAT IS THE GREAT ROTATION?** 

The term "Great Rotation" describes a sustained, structural reallocation of institutional capital between sectors of the economy. Unlike cyclical sector rotations driven purely by valuation, the current rotation is being driven by a confluence of macro forces that are unlikely to reverse in the near term: resurgent inflation, a geopolitical reorientation toward hard power and resource security, and the single largest infrastructure investment cycle since the US Interstate Highway System. 

Our tracking of 152 US industry groups reveals that while the S\&P 500 headline index trades near all-time highs, more industries (64) are in confirmed declining patterns than are in climbing patterns (55). The index's strength is being carried by a concentrated set of industries — predominantly commodities, infrastructure, AI semiconductors, and defence — while the majority of the index quietly deteriorates.   
An investor holding a passive index fund "owns" both outcomes simultaneously: the 523% gain in a Micron and the 54% loss in a Nike. The index gives you the average. Selective, fundamental investment gives you the choice of which side you want to be on. 

**MACRO DRIVERS OF THE ROTATION** 

**Driver 1 — Resurgent Inflation.** CPI running at approximately 3.8% — nearly double the Federal Reserve's stated 2% target — provides the structural underpinning for the rotation. Inflation favours real asset owners and punishes holders of long-duration financial assets. Producers of gold, uranium, copper, and hydrocarbons are the canonical inflation beneficiaries. Energy costs alone were up approximately 18% year-on-year as of early 2026, meaning energy companies are printing cash while consumer-facing businesses absorb margin compression.

**Driver 2 — Negative Real Wages.** Real wages — nominal wage growth adjusted for inflation — are negative by approximately 0.3%. This is a silent tax on the consumer. Consumer-facing industries (retail, advertising, leisure, apparel) face structurally weakening demand. The shoe companies, advertising conglomerates, and professional services firms on the wrong side of this rotation are not suffering from bad management; they are suffering from an adverse macro environment. When the consumer is squeezed, discretionary spending contracts first. 

**Driver 3 — Near-Maximum Institutional Allocation.** The NAAIM Exposure Index — which surveys professional money managers on their equity allocation — recently touched 97 out of 100\. Ninety seven percent of professional capital is already in the market. This does not mean the market falls; it means that the marginal driver of returns is no longer the arrival of new money but the reallocation of existing money. WHERE institutional capital moves matters more than WHETHER it enters equities. That "where" is sectors covered in this report. 

**Driver 4 — The AI Infrastructure Supercycle.** Hyperscaler capital expenditure on AI data centres — from Microsoft, Amazon, Google, and Meta — collectively exceeds $200 billion annually, and this figure is rising. Every data centre requires chips, storage, cooling systems, electrical infrastructure, and physical construction. This creates a multi-year demand chain that flows through every AI-adjacent name in Part II of this report: from custom silicon (AVGO) to storage media (STX) to the physical construction firms that build the facilities (FIX, PWR).   
**Driver 5 — The Defence and Reshoring Supercycle.** Global defence budgets are at multi decade highs. Europe is rearming for the first time since the Cold War. The US CHIPS Act and Inflation Reduction Act have seeded a multi-decade domestic manufacturing revival. America is rebuilding its industrial base — semiconductor fabs, battery plants, EV factories — and the companies that install the electrical infrastructure, wiring, and mechanical systems for these facilities are growing faster than any period since the post-WWII industrial boom.

This framework is specifically well-suited to the current environment because inflation rewards operational efficiency: companies with high gross margins can absorb input cost pressures that would be fatal to commodity margin businesses.

**MACRO & SYSTEMIC RISKS** 

**RECESSION RISK:** A global economic recession would impair earnings across most of the stocks in this report. Commodity prices typically fall in recessions, reducing margins for NEM, CCJ, FCX, and TTE. Infrastructure spending can be deferred, impacting PWR and FIX backlogs. Technology capex by hyperscalers could be reduced, impacting AVGO and STX. 

**INTEREST RATE RISK:** Higher interest rates increase the cost of capital for all companies, potentially compressing valuation multiples even when earnings remain strong. Companies with elevated debt levels (AVGO, FCX, PWR) are more sensitive to rate changes. 

**CHINA ECONOMIC SLOWDOWN:** China is the world's largest consumer of copper, a significant consumer of energy, and the largest growth market for semiconductors and storage. A material Chinese economic slowdown would negatively affect FCX, TTE, AVGO, and STX. 

**INFLATION REVERSAL:** The Great Rotation thesis is partially built on a sustained inflationary environment. A rapid return to low inflation would remove the commodity tailwind for NEM, CCJ, FCX, and TTE, though it would benefit consumers and consumer-facing sectors that are currently on the losing side of the rotation. 

**GEOPOLITICAL RISK:** Escalation of geopolitical tensions can disrupt supply chains, restrict commodity exports, and create operational risk for companies with international assets. This is a double-edged risk: it supports defence spending (RKLB) and gold prices (NEM) while creating operational risk for commodity producers in emerging markets. 

**SECTOR-LEVEL RISKS**   
**COMMODITY PRICE CYCLES:** Gold, uranium, copper, and energy prices are inherently cyclical. The current upswing has structural drivers, but all commodities eventually cycle. A reversal from current elevated levels would materially impair the financial performance of NEM, CCJ, FCX, and TTE simultaneously — reducing the diversification benefit of owning all four. 

**AI CAPEX CYCLE:** The AI infrastructure buildout is real, but it is also a capital expenditure cycle — and capital expenditure cycles eventually moderate. A slowdown in hyperscaler AI investment would simultaneously impact AVGO, FIX (data centre construction), STX (AI storage), and PWR (data centre grid connections). The portfolio has meaningful concentration in this theme. 

**POLICY RISK:** US domestic infrastructure spending (IRA, CHIPS Act, grid modernisation) is a significant driver for FIX and PWR. Political changes that reduce or redirect this spending could impair their growth trajectories. 

**DEFENCE BUDGET RISK:** While global defence budgets are growing, any significant reduction in US defence spending (sequestration) could affect RKLB's government contract awards. 

**VALUATION & MARKET RISKS** 

**VALUATION MULTIPLES:** Several stocks in this report trade at elevated multiples reflecting their growth trajectories. If growth disappoints or the market re-rates growth stocks to lower multiples, prices could fall even if earnings are maintained. 

**SECTOR ROTATION REVERSAL:** The Great Rotation could itself rotate. If macro conditions normalise (inflation returns to 2%, rates decline, consumer spending recovers), capital could flow back to consumer-facing and rate-sensitive sectors, potentially at the expense of the stocks in this report.
