**5\. THE “FOREVER SQUEEZE” — LOCKUP MECHANICS** 

**Setup.** The S-1 describes a two-track lockup architecture on pages 266 to 267 of the filing. We view this as the single most important pre-listing structural feature for investors to understand, because it dictates how much stock can actually trade in the months following the offering. 

**Track A — Musk and significant investors.** Shares held by Elon Musk and a list of significant investors are subject to a 366-day lockup. The filing contains no early-release provisions for this track. In practice, this means a large portion of the company's pre-IPO equity is removed from the tradable float for approximately one calendar year. 

**Track B — other insiders.** Other insiders are subject to a 180-day base lockup with a series of staggered early-release triggers. The triggers, as disclosed, include: 20% release after the second-quarter post IPO earnings report; an additional 10% if the stock closes 30% above the IPO price for five out of ten trading days; further tranches of approximately 7% releasing on days 70, 90, 105, 120, and 135; 28% release after third-quarter post-IPO earnings; and any remaining balance at day 180\.

**5.1 NASDAQ FAST-ENTRY RULE** 

Nasdaq's listing-rule update effective May 1, 2026, reduced the index-seasoning period to 15 days, eliminated the prior minimum float requirement, and introduced a 3× weighting factor for issuers whose public float is below 20%. The change has been widely reported by Reuters and major financial-news outlets. Practically, the rule accelerates the timeline by which a newly listed mega-cap can be added to passive-index portfolios. 

**ANALYST NOTE** 

The “forever squeeze” is a description of mechanics, not a forecast of returns. Many large IPOs with locked-up floats have experienced violent rallies followed by violent unwinds when lockups release. Investors should anchor on the lockup calendar published in the S-1.

**First-time disclosure.** The S-1 contains a written commitment to deliver “non-Earth-based data centers capable of delivering 100 terawatts of compute per year.” This is, to our knowledge, the first time the orbital-data-center concept has been written into a public-company filing of this magnitude. 

**Why orbit.** Three physical advantages support the rationale: (i) continuous solar irradiation in suitable orbits provides effectively free power, (ii) the vacuum of space provides a thermal sink that materially reduces cooling cost relative to Earth-bound hyperscale data centers, and (iii) the absence of grid connection constraints and land-use permits removes the bottlenecks that currently slow data-center construction on the ground. 

**Capex implications.** Reaching the 100 TW capacity goal will require lift capacity that does not currently exist in commercial form. Our analysis suggests the unit launch economics of next-generation, fully reusable heavy-lift vehicles are the binding constraint. 

**REALITY CHECK** 

The 100 terawatt figure is a roadmap target, not a near-term revenue line. Investors should treat it as the upper bound of management's ambition, scaffolded by the CEO compensation milestones described in Section 9\. 

**Why this matters.** Compensation milestones that extend to multi-trillion-dollar market capitalizations communicate two things: (i) the founder is structurally aligned with very long-duration outcomes, and (ii) the founder has personal incentive to manage capital structure—possibly including share-backed borrowing —to avoid premature liquidation of his vested stake.

**15\. HISTORICAL PATTERN — WHAT MEGA-IPOS TEACH US** 

**The cautionary list.** History shows that the largest, most-hyped IPOs are not automatically good investments at the open print. Uber, Rivian, Snap, and Facebook each priced into significant retail enthusiasm and then traded substantially lower than   
interesting public offering of the current cycle.
