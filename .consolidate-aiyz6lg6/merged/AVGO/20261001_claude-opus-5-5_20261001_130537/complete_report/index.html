The core thesis¶

1. AVGO is a picks-and-shovels play on AI, with real switching costs. Broadcom co-designs custom AI accelerators (XPUs) with hyperscalers and also supplies the Ethernet switching and connectivity silicon that ties AI clusters together. Custom silicon is not a commodity purchase. Once a hyperscaler builds its software stack, compiler toolchain, and datacenter architecture around a chip generation, switching partners means years of re-engineering. That makes AVGO's design wins sticky, and the work spans multiple generations.

2. The software segment gives AVGO a floor most semiconductor peers lack. The VMware acquisition added a large, high-margin, recurring-revenue base. Cyclical chip companies usually can't offer that kind of cash-flow stability, and it should dampen the effect of semiconductor downturns on consolidated earnings.

3. Management's operating model has a long track record. Hock Tan's playbook is to buy mission-critical franchises, cut the cost structure, focus on the largest customers, and compound free cash flow. It has worked repeatedly. AVGO has also paired it with a long record of dividend growth and debt paydown after large deals.

Where I expect the bear to push, and my response¶

"The valuation already prices in perfection." Premium multiples are fair to flag, but a multiple is only expensive relative to the durability and growth of the earnings behind it. If AI revenue keeps compounding while software cash flows provide ballast, earnings growth can compress the multiple without the stock needing to fall. The question is whether the growth runway is longer than the market assumes, and design-win visibility spanning several years argues it may be.

"Customer concentration is dangerous." It is a real risk, but it cuts both ways. A small number of hyperscalers with enormous capex budgets and deeply integrated designs is a concentrated base, and it is also a high-barrier, high-visibility one. The bear needs to show that a major customer is likely to defect, not just that one could.

"AI capex will digest and revenue will roll over." Capex cycles do pause. But custom silicon is increasingly tied to hyperscalers' own cost-per-inference economics, so these programs are strategic, not discretionary. If spending slows, I'd expect the XPU programs that lower customers' unit costs to be among the last cut.

"Custom XPU and system-level sales dilute gross margins." I'd concede that mix shift can pressure gross margin percentage. But gross margin percentage isn't the right target. Operating profit dollars and free cash flow per share are, and a large, growing revenue stream at slightly lower margin can still be strongly accretive.

"VMware customers will revolt over pricing." Some churn and pushback is plausible, and it's worth monitoring. But enterprise virtualization is deeply embedded and painful to migrate off, so the likelier outcome is gradual erosion at the margin rather than a collapse of the franchise.

"Competitors and insourcing will take share." Marvell, Nvidia's own custom offerings, and hyperscalers' in-house teams are all real threats. But AVGO's combination of SerDes, packaging, networking IP, and execution at scale is hard to replicate, and "could be disrupted" isn't evidence of disruption.

What would change my mind¶

  • A major hyperscaler publicly moving its flagship accelerator program to another partner
  • Sustained, steep gross margin decline that outpaces operating leverage
  • VMware renewal rates deteriorating meaningfully
  • Evidence of AI infrastructure spend being cut, not just delayed
  • Leverage rising again without a clear deleveraging path

Bottom line¶

The bull position is that AVGO combines a secular growth engine (AI custom silicon and networking) with a cash-generating annuity (infrastructure software) under a disciplined capital allocator. That mix is unusual in semiconductors.
