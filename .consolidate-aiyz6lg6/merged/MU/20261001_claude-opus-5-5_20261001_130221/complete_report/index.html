The core problem: MU is a commodity cycle, and the bull case is always "this time is different"¶

Every memory upcycle comes with a story about why the old boom-and-bust pattern is dead. In 2018 it was cloud and data centers. In 2021 it was pandemic digitization. Today it's AI and HBM. After each one, MU reported a multi-billion-dollar loss (fiscal 2023 is the most recent example), took inventory write-downs, and cut capex and headcount. The stock has repeatedly looked cheapest on P/E at peak earnings, right before the cycle turned. If the bull's argument leans on forward earnings multiples, that is the trap.

1. Supply response is the thing that kills memory margins¶

The bull will say supply discipline is better this time. But the industry is spending heavily on HBM capacity, new fabs, and US and Asian expansions, much of it subsidized. Capacity added in 2026 and 2027 arrives just as demand growth normalizes. Chinese suppliers like CXMT and YMTC keep scaling in the segments where they can compete, which pressures conventional DRAM and NAND pricing. These conventional products are still a large share of MU's revenue [verify mix]. HBM's wafer-intensity may tighten the market for now, but it also raises the stakes of overbuilding.

2. HBM is a good business, not a moat¶

  • Three suppliers, one dominant buyer. Nvidia and the hyperscalers can dual- or triple-source, and they negotiate hard. SK Hynix has been the HBM leader, and Samsung has the balance sheet to buy share with price if it clears qualification.
  • Each generation resets the race. HBM4 brings custom base dies, tighter logic integration, and more reliance on foundry partners. Any qualification slip or yield problem can cost MU share for a full product cycle.
  • "Sold out" is not "priced forever." Volume and pricing commitments are typically set annually. They protect near-term revenue, not margins two years out.

3. Demand is concentrated in a small number of AI capex budgets¶

MU's marginal profit growth is tied to a handful of hyperscaler and accelerator customers. If AI capex digests, with a pause in GPU orders, power or datacenter bottlenecks, or financing strain among AI buyers, HBM and server DRAM demand can reverse quickly, and memory inventories build fast. Meanwhile the consumer side (phones, PCs) has not been a reliable growth offset [verify current demand trends]. Double-ordering and customer inventory building are classic late-cycle signals, and they're hard to see until the quarter after the damage.

4. Cash flow is consumed by the business model¶

Memory requires constant, enormous reinvestment. Free cash flow is thin or negative in the investment phase of the cycle, and capex commitments are sticky when pricing turns. The dividend is token and buybacks are discretionary. Shareholders get the volatility while the fabs absorb the cash. [verify trailing FCF and capex guidance]

5. Geopolitical and policy exposure is not hypothetical¶

  • China has already restricted MU in critical infrastructure, and the risk of further retaliation tied to export controls is ongoing.
  • Manufacturing is concentrated in Taiwan, Japan, and other parts of Asia, which carries regional and supply-chain risk.
  • US fab buildouts bring cost and timeline risk. CHIPS Act support offsets some cost, but it doesn't guarantee returns on those fabs.
  • Tariff and export-control changes can hit the cost structure and customer mix with little notice.
